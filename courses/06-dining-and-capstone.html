Part 1 · covers seating, paying and toasts

At the table

5 min live

A business dinner in China is often at a round table, and the seats are not all equal. The seat facing the door is widely described as the seat of honour, 主位 (zhǔwèi, seat of honour); the side that invited is the host and usually pays; and during the meal people stand or lean in to 敬酒 (jìngjiǔ, propose a toast) to one another, often with 干杯 (gānbēi, cheers, bottoms up). All three are widely described practice rather than sourced rules: the sources behind these pages do not document them, accounts of seating disagree in the details, and every one varies by region, by company and by the people at the table.

LiveWhere to sit, and who pays2 min▶

The seat. The seat facing the door, 主位 (zhǔwèi, seat of honour), is widely described as the most important place at the table. Accounts disagree on who takes it: some describe the host sitting there with the chief guest beside them, others describe the chief guest being placed there. The junior people on the host side are usually described as sitting near the door, closest to the waiters. None of this comes from a source behind these pages, and the habit varies by region and by company. The move that works everywhere is the same: arrive, greet, and wait for the host to show you your seat.

The bill. The side that invites, the side that 请客 (qǐngkè, treats, hosts a meal), is the host and usually pays. As Lumen is the guest tonight, Lumen does not reach for the bill. A guest thanks the host and offers to return the dinner, for example with 下次我请 (xiàcì wǒ qǐng, next time is on me). This is common practice, not a sourced rule; in some companies and some regions it runs differently, and a host who insists on paying is following the ritual in Part 2.

Live敬酒 (jìngjiǔ, propose a toast), 干杯 (gānbēi, cheers, bottoms up), and a polite decline2 min▶

At many business dinners people take turns to 敬酒 (jìngjiǔ, propose a toast) to someone at the table, often starting with the host, and say 干杯 (gānbēi, cheers, bottoms up), which literally means "dry the cup". These are widely described habits, not sourced rules, and how much alcohol is involved varies a great deal by region, by company and by generation.

SayWhen
敬您一杯 (jìng nín yì bēi, a toast to you)Raising your glass to one person, usually someone senior.
祝我们合作愉快 (zhù wǒmen hézuò yúkuài, here is to a happy partnership)The content of a business toast: the partnership, not the deal terms.
我不太能喝 (wǒ bú tài néng hē, I cannot drink much)Declining alcohol politely, with no reason needed.
以茶代酒 (yǐ chá dài jiǔ, tea in place of wine)Toasting with tea or another drink instead. You still take part in the toast.

The decline is a phrase, not an excuse. 我不太能喝 (wǒ bú tài néng hē, I cannot drink much) followed by 以茶代酒 (yǐ chá dài jiǔ, tea in place of wine) lets you join every toast without drinking. These phrases are common usage that any business speaker will recognise, not a finding from a published source.

A note on the pinyin: 不太 (bú tài, not very) and 一杯 (yì bēi, one cup) are written with the tone you say, because 不 (bù, not) becomes bú before a fourth tone and 一 (yī, one) becomes yì before a first tone. Standard pinyin (GB/T 16159-2012) writes the original tone and allows the changed tone to be written in teaching, which is what these pages do.

Self-studyWhy every one of these varies2 min▶

Descriptions of Chinese business dining are generalisations about a country of many regions, cuisines and company cultures. A state-owned enterprise in the north, a tech start-up in Shenzhen and a family distributor in Shanghai may host the same guest in three different ways, and a younger team may skip the toasts entirely. Writers on etiquette rarely agree on the fine details of seating, which is why the figure leaves the question of who takes seat 1 open.

So hold these habits the way session 5 held face: as tendencies to watch for, not predictions. Watch where the host points, notice whether people are toasting with alcohol or with tea, and follow. If you are unsure, asking the person beside you is not a mistake; taking the seat of honour uninvited is the guess most likely to cause an awkward moment.

Part 2 · covers ritual refusal and insistence

客气 (kèqi, standing on ceremony)

5 min live

客气 (kèqi, standing on ceremony) is politeness shown by holding back: declining the first offer of food, the better seat or the bill, so that the host can insist. The host's side of it is 别客气 (bié kèqi, please do not stand on ceremony). The exchange is widely described rather than sourced, and how many rounds it runs varies by region, company and person, but the shape is easy to hear once you know it: an offer, a refusal, an insistence, and then an acceptance.

LiveThe exchange, line by line3 min▶
BeatYou hear or sayWhat it does
Offer再来一点 (zài lái yìdiǎn, have a little more)The host offers, often more than once.
Refuse不用了，不用了 (bú yòng le, bú yòng le, no need, really)The guest holds back. The first refusal is often ritual, not a real no.
Insist别客气 (bié kèqi, please do not stand on ceremony)The host shows the welcome is real.
Accept那我就不客气了 (nà wǒ jiù bú kèqi le, then I will not stand on ceremony)The guest accepts and the ritual ends.
Thank您太客气了 (nín tài kèqi le, you are too kind)Said to a host who has gone out of their way.

This is widely described practice, not a sourced rule, and the phrases are common usage. The number of rounds is the part that varies most: some hosts offer once and mean it, some expect two refusals. Follow the host. If they offer a second time with the same words, accept.

Self-studyThe same three beats at the bill, and how it links to session 52 min▶

At the bill. Even when the host has clearly invited, a guest may make a show of reaching for the bill, say 我来 (wǒ lái, let me, I will get it), and let the host insist. That is the ritual, not a real attempt to pay; actually paying, as in the Real world case in Part 1, cuts the host's line off. When the host insists, thank them, and offer 下次我请 (xiàcì wǒ qǐng, next time is on me). All of this is common practice, and it varies.

The link to the meeting room. Session 5's softened refusals, such as 再考虑考虑 (zài kǎolǜ kǎolǜ, let us think about it more), and the ritual refusal at dinner sound alike and mean opposite things. At the table, the first no usually invites an insistence. In the meeting room, a softened no usually is a no, and insisting turns it into a public one. The difference is the setting, and the open follow-up questions from session 5 are still the safe move across the table.

The capstone, and the negotiation ship gate

5 min live

The capstone is one constructed day in the running case, run end to end: the morning meeting where the price is settled, the formal email that records it, the WeChat that confirms it and the dinner that celebrates it. Each moment draws on one or two earlier sessions. You write all of it in the build-along, then grade it here on eight rows, each scored 0, 1 or 2. The gate is strict on purpose: a deal with a missing date, or an email in the wrong register, is the kind of small fault that costs trust in the next round.

Self-studyWhy row 5 is the strictest2 min▶

Graham and Lam, in "The Chinese Negotiation" (Harvard Business Review, October 2003), write that "a broken promise or display of anger or aggression causes mutual loss of face". A next step with a date is a promise. So row 5 asks for two things, not one: a date, and a date you have already cleared on your own side. A 2 on row 5 with a date you cannot keep is worse than a 1.

Every growth figure in my numbers update carries its comparison word, 同比 (tóngbǐ, year on year) or 环比 (huánbǐ, period on period), used correctly, and I checked each percentage against its two inputs. Session 4. The containers are up 12.5 percent year on year and down 10 percent on the previous quarter; said without the word, the room can hear the opposite story.
When Manager Wang says 有一定难度 (yǒu yídìng nándù, it is somewhat difficult), I read it as a probable no and answer with an open question, not "so is that a yes?". Session 5. Pressing for a yes on the spot turns a softened no into a public one.
Every concession I make is traded, inside 如果 ... 的话 (rúguǒ ... de huà, if ..., in that case), for something back. Session 5. A cut offered for nothing tells the other side the first price had room in it.
The meeting ends on a next step with an owner and a date I have already cleared on my side. Sessions 5 and 6. A date is a promise, and Graham and Lam's line is about broken promises.

The toast敬您一杯 (jìng nín yì bēi, a toast to you) · 祝我们合作愉快 (zhù wǒmen hézuò yúkuài, here is to a happy partnership) · 干杯 (gānbēi, cheers, bottoms up).
The decline我不太能喝 (wǒ bú tài néng hē, I cannot drink much), then 以茶代酒 (yǐ chá dài jiǔ, tea in place of wine). You still toast.
客气 (kèqi, standing on ceremony)Offer, refuse, insist with 别客气 (bié kèqi, please do not stand on ceremony), accept. Follow the host on how many rounds.
